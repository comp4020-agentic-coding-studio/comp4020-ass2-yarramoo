# Policies and support

## Late work

Each checkpoint and the final project has a fixed due date, shown on its
[assessment page](/assessments/). Work submitted after the deadline loses 5%
of the available weight per day late, up to five days, after which it is
marked at zero. There is no penalty-free grace window — a checkpoint that
builds on the previous one's working code is not useful to mark out of order,
so the schedule is designed around the due dates holding, not around students
guessing how much slack exists.

## Extensions

Extensions are granted for documented illness or misadventure, requested
before the deadline where at all possible. Ask your tutor directly rather than
waiting for a lecture; a short email is enough to start the conversation.
Extensions on Checkpoint 3 are considered more carefully than the others,
because the final project starts from Checkpoint 3's codebase — a late
Checkpoint 3 compresses the final project's timeline too, and that's worth
discussing explicitly rather than discovering in week 12.

## Academic integrity, and the CSNOBOL4 convention

The interpreter you submit at each checkpoint has to be your own work: your
own lexer, your own parser, your own evaluator, written to the brief. That
does not mean you write it in the dark. This course explicitly encourages
**differential testing against CSNOBOL4** — the actively-maintained reference
implementation of SNOBOL4 that descends from the original Bell Labs
compiler — as a way of checking your own output, not as a source to copy
from. If your interpreter and CSNOBOL4 disagree on a program that the
language reference doesn't call out as ambiguous or implementation-defined,
that disagreement is a bug in your code, and finding it that way is normal
practice, not misconduct.

The line is this: running a program through CSNOBOL4 to see what the correct
answer is, and then writing your own code to produce that answer, is
legitimate testing. Copying or transcribing CSNOBOL4's own source (or anyone
else's interpreter, including another student's) into your submission is
not. If you use any AI assistance beyond what's normal in an IDE
(autocomplete, inline diagnostics), declare what you used and how in your
submission — a line or two is enough. Undeclared assistance is treated the
same as any other undeclared source.

## Getting help

Studios are the first place to get unstuck — a tutor is in the room and has
usually seen the exact `match` exhaustiveness error you're looking at before.
Outside studio hours, email reaches the person named on each
[people page](/people/): course-level questions to the convenor, everything
about a specific checkpoint or studio to the tutor. There's no separate
office-hours booking system — an email gets a reply or a time to talk.
