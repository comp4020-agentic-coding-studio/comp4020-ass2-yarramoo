## Bell Labs, 1962

Holmdel, New Jersey. A department working on high-level, nonnumerical
computation.

**David J. Farber, Ralph E. Griswold, Ivan P. Polonsky** needed a better
tool for manipulating symbolic expressions in their own research —
and built one, rather than waiting for one to exist.

---

## Not a product. A tool that got used.

SNOBOL was not commissioned. It grew out of what its own authors needed
for their own work, then kept getting used because it kept working.

```notes
Worth pausing on: this is the opposite origin story to most languages
students will have met. Not designed top-down against a spec.
```

---

## SNOBOL4: the fourth language, not the fourth patch

- designed from 1966, running by 1967
- unlimited-length strings
- **pattern matching promoted to a first-class data type**
- arrays, tables
- SIL: a portable implementation strategy — a hypothetical assembly
  language for an abstract machine

---

## SIL bought SNOBOL4 reach

Write the interpreter against a small virtual instruction set, not any
one machine's assembler.

Result: roughly **fifty machines and operating systems** by the late
1970s.

```notes
Same trick this course reaches for if the compiler ever targets a
bytecode VM instead of native code. Foreshadowing, not a promise.
```

## The naming story — two tellings, neither fully verifiable

**Griswold's own account**: he coined "SNOBOL," then reverse-engineered
"StriNg Oriented symBOlic Language" afterward, as a joke.

**Farber's account**: a "snowball's chance in hell" moment, over coffee.

---

## Why this course takes no side

Both tellers were there. They disagree on the *mechanism*, not just the
colour.

A small, concrete lesson about oral history vs. primary documents — and
it matters more to how you read sources than the specific fact does.

```notes
One thing every account agrees on, worth saying out loud: SNOBOL is not
related to COBOL. Deliberate wordplay, no other connection.
```

## What makes SNOBOL4 different

A "build a compiler for a C-like language" course teaches you a lot.
It does not teach you this:

**pattern matching as a first-class, backtracking data type** — not
syntax bolted onto a regular-expression engine.

## Why OCaml

Two structural properties of SNOBOL4 map directly onto language
features, not library support.

---

## Property one: success/failure is the only branch

No `if`. No `while`. No `for`.

Every SNOBOL4 statement succeeds or fails — that signal, not a boolean,
is the language's only control-flow primitive.

**This is what a sum type is built to express.**

---

## Property two: every value is dynamically tagged

A variable can hold a string, an integer, a real, a pattern, an array, a
table, or a programmer-defined record.

In OCaml: one variant type, one constructor per SNOBOL type — the
compiler checks every place that inspects a value handles every case.

```notes
And it mirrors the real historical implementation: SNOBOL4's own runtime
represented every value as a tagged descriptor, for the same reason.
```

---

## Property three (arriving week 7): backtracking is a recursive interpreter

Try an alternative; on failure, unwind and try the next one.

A standard shape for a small recursive interpreter in a language with
pattern matching and sum types built in.
